The invisible resistance that halts progress

By Nikesh Lagun

If your task doesn’t “fit,” your brain can’t grip. Learn how to fix Flexion.

Share

Post on X ↗LinkedIn ↗Copy link ↗

Hey friends,

There are days when even the smallest task feels like a mountain.

Not because it’s hard. Not because you don’t know how. But because... something just doesn’t click.

Maybe it’s replying to an email. Or filling out a simple form. Or reading a page you’ve read three times already. The task is right there in front of you, clear and manageable, but your brain slides right off it. No grip. No momentum. Just a strange, silent resistance.

This isn’t laziness. It’s not procrastination. It’s something else entirely.

It’s a thing called Flexion.

I want to do it. I just… can’t seem to get in.

Flexion isn’t a word you’ll find in most productivity books. But it’s central to a fascinating new field in cognitive psychology called Cognitive Drive Architecture, or CDA. This isn’t about motivation hacks or daily routines. It’s a deeper dive into the structural side of effort. Not what you feel about the task, but how your system is configured to interact with it.

At the heart of CDA is a framework known as Lagunian Dynamics. It treats effort as something structural, not emotional. In this view, what we call “Drive,” your readiness to act, is not a mood or mindset. It’s an outcome. It emerges from the interaction of six core variables. And one of the most pivotal is Flexion.

So what exactly is it?

Flexion is the fit between the task and your current cognitive state. It’s not about how difficult the task is. It’s about how grippable it feels. That subtle click when a task aligns with your energy, your attention, and your mental shape in the moment.

You know the feeling when it’s there. You sit down, and even if the task is complex, it flows. And when it’s not? Even a simple action feels weirdly foreign.

That’s Flexion at play. And when Flexion is low, the whole system can stall out.

Here’s how CDA breaks it down:

It's called Lagun’s Law, and it tells us how Drive is formed. When Flexion drops, regardless of how motivated, smart, or disciplined you are, Drive can collapse.

And that’s exactly what it feels like when people say,

I know what to do. I just can’t seem to do it.

I’m not tired. But I feel... locked out.

It’s not that you're unwilling. It’s that your system can't grip.

Why does this happen?

Several things can throw off Flexion. Some are small. Others are surprisingly subtle.

  • The task is too abstract, or not abstract enough, for your current mental mode.

  • It lacks emotional tone; your brain is looking for something resonant, and this feels flat.

  • You’re in idea mode, but the task needs precision. That mismatch creates drag.

  • There’s unprocessed clutter in the background; loose threads and unresolved to-dos can make your mental space too noisy for the task to land.

  • Or maybe… It’s just the wrong time of day. Some tasks simply don’t fit the current hour.

When these things are true, the task becomes rigid. It doesn’t meet you where you are. And just like a stiff shoe, you can’t run in it.

But here’s the good news:

Flexion is not fixed. It bends. And we can help it bend.

A few quiet strategies can make a big difference:

  • Reframe the shape. Instead of “write a report,” try “jot down three things I want someone to know.” A slight shift in framing can increase grip.

  • Shift state first. Don’t muscle through misalignment. Try a small warm-up: review a related note, listen to a snippet of relevant audio, or just imagine finishing.

  • Make it feel new. Novelty boosts Flexion. Switch the medium, the format, and the space. Even tiny changes can refresh the task’s surface.

  • Clear the mental table. If background noise is high, offload it. Write down the clutter. Name the loose ends. A clearer field invites a better fit.

  • Shrink the entry point. Make the start smaller. Five-minute slices. Micro-goals. Flexion rises when the structure is something you can wrap your mind around.

And maybe just as important, what Flexion is not:

It is not laziness.

It is not a failure.

It is not a lack of care or capability.

It’s a structural mismatch.

This distinction matters deeply, especially in educational and clinical contexts. People with ADHD, for example, often feel the sting of the “effort paradox” when motivation and intelligence are high, but action won’t come. CDA shows us that the barrier isn’t desire. It’s a fit.

Before we had words like Flexion, we’d just say, “I don’t know what’s wrong. This feels weird.” Now, there’s language. There’s a shape. There’s a model.

When Flexion is low, don’t assume you need more will.

First, ask: does the task need to bend?

Because when it does, when the structure meets the state, action becomes natural. Effort returns. The system moves.

And that strange stillness, that stuck feeling, begins to ease.

Wishing you a lighter, easier week ahead,

Nikesh Lagun
