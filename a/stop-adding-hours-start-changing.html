Stop adding hours. Start changing shape.

By Nikesh Lagun

A new way to think about effort, flexibility, and real progress

Let’s get started…

I want to share something very personal with you today.
Not just as someone who studies how we work,
but as the person who built the very theories I’m about to talk about.

There was a time in my life when I kept doing ten-hour days and still felt like I was getting nowhere.

On paper, I was the perfect example of focus. My calendar was blocked. My notifications were off. I stayed at my desk for long stretches, believing discipline alone would carry me through. Yet when I looked back at my week or my month, or even my year, nothing meaningful had moved forward.

It was confusing, and, if I am honest, it hurt. I believed in what I was doing. I was not lazy. I was not distracted. I was putting in the hours, and still the work refused to budge.

At first, I told myself what so many of us do: work harder, push through, and be more disciplined. But every time I extended my hours or tried to force more focus, I hit the same invisible wall. The task itself did not move.

Over time, as I began developing what is now known as Cognitive Drive Architecture, I started to see what was really happening.

The problem was not my hours.
It was the shape of the task.

We are often told that success is about effort and focus. Keep your head down. Stay on task. Log the hours. That sounds like a recipe for progress.

Except it is not.

Through my research, I have come to see drive and effort not as simple traits, but as a system. Imagine your ability to work as an engine with moving parts: ignition, adaptability, stabilization, and even internal friction. That is the heart of what I call Cognitive Drive Architecture.

One of the theories I created inside this system is Lagunian Dynamics. It shows that whether your effort becomes progress depends on how the six elements interact:
your internal go switch,
your mental activation voltage,
how well the task fits you,
your attention tether,
the resistance you are feeling,
and the turbulence life throws at you.

When those align, work flows. When they do not, you can sit there for ten hours and feel like nothing is happening.

What I eventually discovered is that the task I was working on was too rigid. A task with no flexibility can grind your drive system to a halt. Even when you are motivated and ready, you are pouring energy into something that cannot absorb it.

Another part of my framework, the Cognitive Thermostat Theory, explains that task initiation works like a feedback loop. It is not a simple switch. You can have plenty of motivation and still fail to see results if your task is misaligned with your mental state.

Looking back, I see now that the rigidity of my task was generating resistance and starving adaptability. My drive engine was revving, but the gears were not catching.

If you have ever felt like you were pedaling a bike that is stuck in place, you know exactly what I mean.

So, what does it mean to change the shape of the task?

For me, it meant stepping back and asking new questions.

Could I break this task into smaller steps?

Could I redefine the outcome so it fits how I actually work?

Was there a different entry point that felt lighter?

Instead of spending another week hammering away at the same outline, I sketched a mind map. I jumped to the part that felt alive instead of forcing a linear path. I gave myself permission to prototype and to play.

Almost immediately, the task started to fit my mental state. Progress returned.

There is one more layer I want to share with you.
In my research, I call it Latent Task Architecture. It looks at how background tasks and half-finished ideas quietly drain your drive. Every unfinished email, every vague commitment, every lingering “I should really get to that” adds weight.

When I finally took a look at my week, I saw not just one rigid task but a cloud of half-finished ones around it. Clearing some of that load made the main task feel lighter, like clearing leaves from a path.

Here is what I want you to remember:

You are not broken.
You are not lazy.
You are not bad at focus.

You might simply be facing a task that is structurally misaligned with your cognitive system. No amount of extra hours will fix that.

Take a look at what is in front of you today.

  1. Can the task bend to fit you, or are you bending yourself out of shape to fit it?

  2. Can you clear some background friction that is quietly pulling on your attention?

  3. Can you choose a softer entry point or celebrate a small start instead of pushing through the hard edge?

Progress often returns not because we add hours, but because we remove resistance.

Sometimes success is not about pushing harder.
It is about giving your mind a task it can actually move.

Wishing you a lighter, easier week ahead,

Nikesh Lagun
