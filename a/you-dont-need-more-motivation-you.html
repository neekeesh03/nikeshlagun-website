You don’t need more motivation (You need less grain)

By Nikesh Lagun

Why modern psychology says your stuckness is structural, not personal

Let’s get started…

I’ve had mornings where I open my laptop, sit down with a warm cup of coffee, a clear to-do list, and a full night’s sleep, and still, nothing happens.

Not because I didn’t want to work. Not because I wasn’t motivated.

Just… nothing.

It took me years of both lived experience and research to understand what was happening. I wasn’t tired in the usual way. I wasn’t burnt out. I wasn’t lazy.

I was carrying something invisible.

We call it Grain.

Grain is mental resistance. It’s the drag that builds inside your system and quietly blocks effort before it even begins. It doesn’t yell. It doesn’t panic. It just slows things down until your clearest goals feel strangely far away.

Even the things you care about start to feel heavier than they should.

Here’s the hopeful part: Grain isn’t some mysterious mood or personality flaw. It’s a real part of how Drive works, and once you learn to see it, you can start reducing it.

I work in a field of cognitive psychology called Cognitive Drive Architecture, or CDA. It doesn’t study motivation the way traditional psychology does. It looks deeper. It asks what effort is actually made of, how it forms, fails, or sustains based on internal structure, not just emotion.

At the core of CDA is a theory I developed called Lagunian Dynamics. It’s a structural model, a kind of blueprint, for Drive. It maps out six core variables that shape your ability to start and stay with a task.

They include things like:

  • Primode (your ignition switch)

  • CAP (how much startup energy you have)

  • Flexion (how well a task fits your current mental state)

  • Anchory (your attention tether)

  • Slip (random disruptions)

  • And, of course, Grain

Grain is one of the most critical. When it builds, it cancels out everything else: your energy, your attention, and your task alignment.

It doesn’t care how important the task is. It only knows that something inside your system isn’t ready yet.

Grain often shows up quietly. You might feel slightly off. You start, then stop. You reread the same sentence. You keep bouncing between tabs. You tell yourself you’ll try again in ten minutes. That turns into an hour. Then a day.

You blame yourself. You try harder. But that just creates more pressure and more Grain.

What I’ve found is that trying to push through resistance like this almost never works. Not because you’re weak, but because you’re working against your system instead of with it.

Grain tends to rise in specific conditions:

  • When the task feels emotionally heavy

  • When your mental environment is cluttered

  • When you’re overloaded with open loops

  • When you’ve framed the work in a way that feels rigid or too important to “mess up”

In another model within CDA, called Latent Task Architecture, we talk about Latent Load: the hidden, background clutter your mind is carrying. This includes things like unread messages, half-finished work, and unresolved decisions. Even if you’re not actively thinking about them, they sit there like static, slowly increasing Grain.

That’s why a task that looks simple on paper can feel impossible when you’re carrying too much in the background.

It’s not the task. It’s the drag underneath it.

The good news? Grain isn’t permanent. It’s dynamic. It shifts with configuration. And that means it can be reduced without needing to push harder or hustle more.

When I notice Grain showing up in my own system, these are the small interventions I return to again and again:

  • First, I name it.
    The moment I feel resistance, I pause and ask:

“Is this tiredness, or is this Grain?”
Just that question interrupts the cycle of blame.

  • Then, I shrink the task.
    If something feels too big to start, I cut it down to something so small it’s almost silly.

Not “finish the report.”
Just “open the document.”
Just “write the first line.”
Grain breaks when momentum begins.

  • Next, I clear clutter.
    I write down anything floating in my head: errands, emails, reminders, and random tension points.
    Even one small item resolved lifts the fog more than you’d expect.

  • I also adjust the frame.
    If I’ve labeled something as high-stakes, I reframe it.

“This is just a draft.”
“I’m just trying it out.”
That’s not avoidance. That’s flexibility. And flexibility lowers Grain.

  • Finally, I re-anchor.
    I set a short timer, 10 or 15 minutes.
    I use soft music or ambient sound. I reduce tabs.

I gently tether my attention, instead of forcing it to obey.

Most importantly, I remind myself: this is not a moral issue. It’s a mechanical one. My system isn’t broken. It’s just asking for alignment.

We live in a world that tends to treat effort like a character trait. But it’s not. Drive isn’t something you summon out of thin air. It’s something your system produces when the parts are working together.

If you take one thing with you today, let it be this:

You don’t need more motivation.

You need less Grain.

And when you reduce that drag, you reconnect with the Drive that was always there, waiting to move forward once the path was clear again.

Wishing you a lighter, easier week ahead,

Words in plain English
