The real reason you can’t stay focused (and it’s not motivation).

By Nikesh Lagun

A look at the structural forces inside your mind that tug attention off-course.

Share

Post on X ↗LinkedIn ↗Copy link ↗

Hey friends,

Focus instability feels personal, but Lagun’s Law shows it is actually structural. Your mind uses a small set of internal “drive switches” that decide how easily you can start, stay with, and return to a task. When these switches drift out of balance, focus slips even if you care, even if you are motivated, and even if the task matters. The problem is not that you are unfocused. The problem is that the system producing focus is wobbling.

The first switch is Primode, your ignition threshold. When Primode is high, starting feels like trying to push a car from a dead halt. You want to go, but your system has not actually “clicked on” yet.

• High Primode → stuck at the starting line
• Lower Primode → smooth entry into action

Right next to it sits CAP, your internal spark. CAP is the burst of energy that helps you launch. But if CAP is too low, you stall. If it spikes too fast, you lurch forward then collapse.

Ignition is not willpower. It is configuration.

Once you begin, Flexion becomes the stabilizer that checks whether the task fits your current mental state. When Flexion is strong, you settle in quickly. When Flexion drops, the task feels weirdly incompatible, like you are trying to run on the wrong rhythm.

→ Good Flexion = easy engagement
→ Low Flexion = fast decay of attention

Then you rely on Anchory to hold your attention in place. Anchory is your tether. When it weakens, your mind drifts even if your intention doesn’t.

• drift mid sentence
• reread lines
• lose your thread for no reason

That drifting feeling is not a flaw. It is Anchory losing tension.

Meanwhile, Grain quietly increases or decreases the friction in the system. Grain is the mental heaviness created by uncertainty, pressure, emotional load, or the sense that a task is “too much.” When Grain rises, focus becomes expensive to maintain.

And finally, there is Slip, the turbulence. Slip is the little mental shake that pushes you off task even when nothing is wrong. Research on moment-to-moment performance patterns shows Slip is a major reason people with ADHD feel inconsistent: the system has more noise, so attention jumps.

Slip perturbs expressed Drive around the structural core.

Focus instability forms when these six switches stop lining up. It happens in tiny steps:

→ Primode is a little high
→ CAP is a little low
→ Flexion mismatches
→ Grain rises
→ Anchory loosens
→ Slip shakes the system
→ focus falls apart

A surprisingly common disruptor is unfinished tasks sitting in the background. Lagun’s Latent Task Architecture shows that every unresolved task adds invisible weight, raising Grain and weakening Anchory before you even start. Your attention becomes unstable because part of your system is already busy.

Once you see focus this way, the whole story changes. Losing attention is not about character. It is about configuration. When Primode, CAP, Flexion, Anchory, Grain, and Slip shift, your focus shifts with them. The engine wobbles, so the output wobbles.

And the best part: engines can be tuned.
