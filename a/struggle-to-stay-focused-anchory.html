Struggle to stay focused? Anchory may be the missing piece in your productivity system.

By Nikesh Lagun

Cognitive Drive Architecture reveals why willpower fails and how Anchory, the tether that keeps your mind on task, can help you stop drifting, start sustaining, and finally finish what you begin.

Share

Post on X ↗LinkedIn ↗Copy link ↗

Hey friends,

Most of us have been there. You sit down with full motivation. You care about the work. You even want to get it done. But minutes later, your attention drifts.

This isn’t laziness. And it’s not a lack of discipline.

It’s a structural problem in how your mind holds effort.

In Cognitive Drive Architecture (CDA), a new field in cognitive psychology, this problem has a name: Anchory.

Anchory is your mind’s tether, the quiet grip that keeps you connected to the task. When Anchory is strong, focus holds. When it’s weak, your attention floats.

Here’s the big picture:

  • CDA is the field. It studies the system that makes effort possible.

  • Lagunian Dynamics is the core theory that defines that field.

  • Within it, six variables shape whether Drive (your ability to start and sustain effort) actually holds.

Those six are:

  1. Primode (ignition switch): can you start?

  2. CAP (mental voltage): how much push you feel.

  3. Flexion (task fit): does it align with your state?

  4. Anchory (tether): how firmly you stay attached.

  5. Grain (resistance): friction, drag, emotional cost.

  6. Slip (noise): moment-to-moment turbulence.

Anchory sits in the “tension” part of this system. Its role is simple: once you begin, it keeps you attached.

Think of it this way:

  • Motivation starts you.

  • Anchory keeps you.

Without Anchory, even strong motivation fizzles.

Here’s how focus slips without it:

  • You launch with energy.

  • Distractions creep in.

  • Anchory collapses.

  • The tether breaks.

  • You fail to re-enter.

This cycle explains why ADHD often feels like a rollercoaster. Anchory is fragile there, which makes bursts of hyperfocus possible but sustained traction difficult.

The good news? Anchory isn’t fixed. You can strengthen it.

Practical ways to build Anchory:

  1. Create return anchors: sticky notes, highlighted text, or a timer ping. Small cues that pull you back.

  2. Practice micro-restarts: notice drift, label it, and return. Each re-entry builds the tether.

  3. Reduce friction (Grain): break tasks down, clear clutter, and close latent loops.

  4. Align with Flexion: match tasks to your energy, change formats, and adjust your environment.

  5. Borrow ADHD tools: structure, novelty injectors, and transition cues. They make Anchory more resilient.

Everyday examples:

  • A student writes an essay with their thesis in front of them.

  • A professional in meetings jots a keyword to snap back.

  • An artist keeps the same playlist and sketchbook as a ritual.

Anchory isn’t abstract; it’s the everyday habits that hold you steady.

Why it matters: willpower fades. Anchory stabilizes. CDA shows effort isn’t just “trying harder.” It’s system configuration. Anchory proves focus isn’t a trait; it’s a variable you can tune.

If Anchory is weak, distraction wins.
If Anchory is strong, you can return again and again.

Start small: pick one anchor today: a sticky note, a timer, or a keyword. Use it when you drift. Over time, you’ll feel the shift: focus holds, effort steadies, and progress smooths out.

That’s the quiet power of Anchory: not forcing yourself, but giving your mind something to hold onto.

Wishing you a lighter, easier week ahead,

Nikesh Lagun
