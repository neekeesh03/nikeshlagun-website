You Don’t Have a Discipline Problem

By Nikesh Lagun

You have an initiation problem, and it changes everything about productivity.

Hey friends,

It’s been a while since I last wrote to you. I’ve decided to shift this newsletter from weekly to monthly, partly so I can focus more on what I send and partly so I don’t overwhelm you.

Also, a small life update: I’ve recently moved to Hangzhou, China. It’s only been a couple of weeks, but it already feels like a big shift. If you happen to be around, feel free to reach out. I’d genuinely love to meet you.

Big changes like this tend to make you notice something simple but important: how hard it actually is to start things.

Most productivity advice begins with a simple assumption: if you are motivated, you will act. It sounds reasonable. If something matters to you, you should naturally move toward it.

But that’s not what actually happens.

You want to work, but you don’t start. You care about the outcome, yet you delay. You even plan exactly what to do, and still, nothing happens. That gap between intention and action is where most people get stuck, and it’s usually misunderstood.

This is not a motivation problem. It’s an initiation problem.

Across different domains, the same pattern keeps showing up: people can genuinely intend to act and still fail to begin. Even more interesting, increasing incentives or urgency doesn’t lead to a smooth increase in action. Instead, behavior stays flat for long periods and then suddenly spikes, especially near deadlines.

Action doesn’t scale gradually. It switches.

So the real question becomes:

What actually determines whether you start?

The Hidden Layer: Readiness

Before any action begins, something invisible happens first. Your system evaluates whether starting is even possible at that moment.

Not whether it’s important. Not whether you care. But whether you are ready or not.

  • When readiness is sufficient, you begin

  • When readiness is low, nothing happens

No matter how motivated you are.

This is what Lagun’s Law captures structurally: action is not continuous; it is gated. You don’t gradually move into work. You cross a threshold, and only then does action become possible.

This is why two days can feel completely different. On one day, you start easily and continue for hours. On the other hand, you struggle just to begin. The difference isn’t discipline. It’s your readiness state.

A Simple Way to Observe Your Pattern

Before trying to improve anything, it helps to see how your system behaves in real life.

Start with something small. Take a task you’ve been avoiding and sit down to do it. Don’t force yourself, just notice what happens between the moment you sit and the moment you actually begin.

For some people, the transition is immediate. For others, there’s hesitation, distraction, or avoidance. That delay is not random. It’s a direct signal of how ready your system is to initiate action.

Now zoom out and look at a bigger pattern, your behavior around deadlines.

Most people don’t work steadily. Instead, the pattern looks like this:

  • long periods of inactivity

  • followed by a sudden burst of action near the end

That’s not just poor planning. It’s threshold behavior. The system stays below the level required for action, and then, as urgency increases, it finally crosses that boundary.

Once you see this, productivity starts to look very different. It’s no longer about pushing harder. It’s about understanding what moves you across that threshold.

The Structure Behind Your Productivity

Your ability to act doesn’t come from a single factor. It emerges from a small set of interacting forces. You don’t need the math; you just need to understand what each one is doing.

Primode (Readiness)

This is the gate. If it’s low, nothing else matters. You can have plans, urgency, and incentives and still not begin.

CAP (Urgency Amplifier)

This is why deadlines work. Urgency doesn’t build smoothly. It amplifies your state and can suddenly push you into action.

Flexion (Ease of Continuation)

Once you start, this determines whether work feels smooth or heavy. High Flexion leads to flow. Low Flexion leads to early drop-off.

Anchory (Stability of Focus)

This keeps you locked in. Without it, you start and drift. With it, you sustain attention.

Grain (Resistance)

This includes confusion, overload, and unclear steps. Grain doesn’t just slow you down—it suppresses your ability to act.

Slip (Variability)

Even when everything is aligned, output fluctuates. Some days are productive, others aren’t. This is part of the system, not a failure.

Your productivity is not about effort alone. It’s about how these forces interact at any moment.

Why Most Productivity Advice Falls Short

Most systems focus on:

  • discipline

  • motivation

  • time management

But they ignore the most important layer:

whether action can begin at all

That’s why you can know exactly what to do and still not do it. The issue isn’t knowledge or intention. It’s that your system hasn’t crossed the threshold required for action.

How to Actually Fix It

Once you understand this, the strategy changes. You stop trying to force action and start shaping the conditions that allow it.

Lower the starting barrier

Instead of committing to large tasks, reduce the entry point:

  • open the file

  • write one line

  • start for one minute

The goal is simple: make starting almost effortless.

Reduce resistance (Grain)

Most people try to increase motivation. A better move is to remove friction:

  • break tasks into smaller steps

  • remove unnecessary decisions

  • define the exact first move

Lower friction is more reliable than higher motivation.

Use urgency intentionally (CAP)

Deadlines work because they amplify action. You can recreate this with:

  • short timers

  • visible countdowns

  • external accountability

But urgency only works if your system is close to ready.

Protect continuation (Anchory)

Once you start, protect that state:

  • eliminate interruptions

  • keep a consistent environment

  • work in uninterrupted blocks

Starting is the hardest part. Continuing should be easier.

Make work easier over time (Flexion)

  • use templates

  • repeat similar tasks

  • build systems

Less thinking means more execution.

Accept variability (Slip)

Instead of asking:

Why am I inconsistent?

Ask:

How fast can I restart?

The Real Model of Productivity

Productivity is not about forcing yourself to work. It’s about aligning the conditions that make action possible.

You don’t gradually move into action.

You cross a threshold.

And when that threshold is crossed, something changes. Work stops feeling forced. It starts feeling natural.

That’s not discipline.

That’s structure working in your favor.

Wishing you a lighter, easier week ahead,

Nikesh Lagun
