The ADHD struggle you don’t see: Motivation vs. Action

By Nikesh Lagun

How your brain’s effort system fails and what you can do to fix it today.

Hey friends,

Have you ever been excited to start something important, yet found yourself stuck when it was time to begin? It’s something many people with ADHD experience.

Despite having strong motivation and clear intentions, action just doesn’t seem to follow. This isn’t a lack of willpower; it’s a deeper issue with how our brains regulate effort, and for those with ADHD, this regulation often doesn’t work as smoothly.

Procrastination happens to all of us, but with ADHD, it’s not just about avoiding a task. The challenge is in turning intention into action. People with ADHD might start a task with enthusiasm, but it quickly fizzles out. This isn’t laziness; it’s a failure of effort regulation. The mind struggles to align the systems needed to get started, stay focused, and keep moving forward.

To better understand this, I created Cognitive Drive Architecture (CDA). CDA introduces a new way of looking at how effort works within the mind. It focuses on six internal variables that need to be in sync for us to act effectively: Primode (ignition threshold), Cognitive Activation Potential (CAP), Flexion (task adaptability), Anchory (attention stability), Grain (internal resistance), and Slip (variability in performance). When these variables are balanced, effort flows naturally. When they’re misaligned, things stall.

For those with ADHD, these systems are often out of sync. For instance, Primode, the mental switch that should trigger action, may remain inactive. Even if someone is fully aware of the task’s importance, their mind doesn’t initiate the required processes. Meanwhile, CAP, which typically amplifies motivation, might not generate enough energy to cross the ignition threshold. This leaves people feeling ready but unable to act. Once a task does get underway, Grain (resistance) can feel heavier than usual, making it harder to stay on track. Flexion (task adaptability) also fails to keep the system aligned, leading to frustration.

This creates a paradox: the motivation is there, but the action never happens. ADHD often leads to a state of motivated inaction, where there’s an intense desire to complete a task but no ability to start it. It’s frustrating and can lead to guilt, but the real issue is misalignment in the cognitive system. When effort does begin, it can feel unstable, with bursts of focus followed by complete disengagement.

So how can we fix this? Cognitive Thermostat Theory (CTT) offers a solution. Think of your mind like a thermostat that needs adjusting. CTT explains that, just like a thermostat regulates temperature, your brain needs to regulate its effort system. When the “cognitive thermostat” is misaligned, tasks stall. But with CTT, we can fine-tune internal systems: adjusting Primode, boosting CAP, and managing Grain so that ignition happens more smoothly.

Another critical aspect to consider is the impact of latent tasks, the ones we haven’t completed but are still mentally holding onto. These unfinished tasks create something called Latent Load (LL), which interferes with the system’s readiness to start new tasks. By addressing latent load, individuals can reduce the mental clutter that keeps them stuck.

By understanding ADHD through this lens, we can shift away from the idea that it’s simply about laziness or poor motivation. The problem lies in the structure of effort regulation. ADHD isn’t a flaw of willpower; it’s a breakdown in cognitive systems. With the right tools, we can realign those systems and make it easier to start and sustain work.

Wishing you a lighter, easier week ahead,

Words in plain English

Primode: how ready you are to start. CAP: your push of willpower. Flexion: how well the task fits you right now. Anchory: how well you can hold your focus. Grain: inner friction and resistance. Slip: day-to-day wobble.
